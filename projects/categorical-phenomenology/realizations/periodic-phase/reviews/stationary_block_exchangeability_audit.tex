\section*{Stationary aligned-block exchangeability forces product atoms}

Let $\mathcal A$ be a finite alphabet, let $m\geq 1$, and let
$X=(X_n)_{n\geq 1}$ be strictly stationary under the one-site shift.
Suppose that the aligned blocks
\[
 B_k=(X_{km+1},\ldots,X_{km+m}),\qquad k\geq0,
\]
form an infinite exchangeable sequence. Let $Q$ be its de Finetti directing
probability measure on $\mathcal A^m$, realized as the almost sure limiting
empirical block law. Write $\mu=\mathcal L(Q)$.

\paragraph{Claim.}
The measure $\mu$ is concentrated on product probability measures on
$\mathcal A^m$. If $\rho(q_1\otimes\cdots\otimes q_m)
=q_2\otimes\cdots\otimes q_m\otimes q_1$, then
$\rho_*\mu=\mu$. No finite-rank assumption is needed for this conclusion.

\paragraph{Proof.}
The case $m=1$ is immediate. For $m\geq2$ define a continuous map on the
simplex of block laws by
\[
 F(q)=q_{2:m}\otimes q_1,
\]
where the output coordinates are ordered as $2,\ldots,m,1$.
Consider shifted blocks
\[
 C_k=(X_{km+2},\ldots,X_{km+m+1}).
\]
Conditionally on $Q=q$, the blocks $B_k$ are iid with law $q$.
The vector $C_k$ combines the last $m-1$ entries of $B_k$ and the first
entry of $B_{k+1}$, so its conditional marginal law is $F(q)$.
Furthermore, the even-indexed $C_k$ are conditionally iid, as are the
odd-indexed $C_k$: each of these two subsequences uses disjoint pairs of
$B$ blocks. For each word $c\in\mathcal A^m$, the conditional strong law
on these two subsequences, and then their combination, gives
\[
 \frac1N\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mathbf1_{\{C_k=c\}}
 \longrightarrow F(Q)(c)\qquad\text{almost surely}.
\]
There are finitely many $c$, so this identifies the entire empirical
measure limit simultaneously.

Strict stationarity gives equality in law between the infinite sequences
$(C_k)_{k\geq0}$ and $(B_k)_{k\geq0}$. In particular their empirical limits
have the same distribution. Consequently
\[
 \mu=F_*\mu.
\]
For $1\leq r\leq m-1$, direct induction gives
\[
 F^r(q)=q_{r+1:m}\otimes q_1\otimes\cdots\otimes q_r,
\]
and therefore
\[
 F^m(q)=q_1\otimes\cdots\otimes q_m.
\]
It follows that $\mu=(F^m)_*\mu$ is concentrated on product laws.
On that locus $F=\rho$, proving the asserted rotation invariance.
\hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Finite-atomic consequence.}
If $\mu=\sum_{a=1}^p w_a\delta_{q_a}$ with all $w_a>0$ and distinct
$q_a$, then cyclic rotation permutes the set of atoms. Thus there is a
unique permutation $\tau$ with $q_{\tau a}=\rho(q_a)$,
$\tau^m=\mathrm{id}$, and $w_{\tau a}=w_a$.
Writing $q_a=\bigotimes_{j=1}^m e_{a,j}$, the one-step emission from atom
$a$ is $e_{a,1}$, followed by deterministic transition $a\mapsto\tau a$.
For row-vector conventions, the symbol matrix is
\[
 A_y=D_y\Pi,\qquad
 (D_y)_{aa}=e_{a,1}(y),\quad
 \Pi_{a,\tau a}=1.
\]
This realizes the original sequence, not merely its aligned block law.
The claim uses the full de Finetti representation: all sites become
conditionally independent with an $m$-periodic list of marginals.

\paragraph{Rank consequence.}
The ordinary Hankel matrix has rank at most $p$ from the preceding
$p$-state realization. The block Hankel is a submatrix of the ordinary
Hankel. It has rank $p$: distinct probability vectors $q_a$ are separated
by polynomials, and the exchangeable block Hankel is the Gram kernel of
coordinate monomials in $L^2(\mu)$. Hence the ordinary rank is exactly $p$.
Conversely, finite block Hankel rank forces finite support of $\mu$ by
the same polynomial-separation argument.

\paragraph{Why stationarity matters.}
Let $Z_0,Z_1,\ldots$ be iid fair bits and set
$X_{2k+1}=X_{2k+2}=Z_k$. The length-two aligned blocks are iid with law
$q=(\delta_{00}+\delta_{11})/2$, which is not a product law.
The process is invariant under shifts by two, but not by one:
$\mathbb P(X_1=X_2)=1$ while $\mathbb P(X_2=X_3)=1/2$.
Thus aligned-block exchangeability alone, even with rank-one block
Hankel, does not imply the claimed phase-product representation.
The word ``stationary'' here must mean one-site strict stationarity,
not merely block stationarity or constancy of one-dimensional marginals.

\paragraph{Scope.}
The proof establishes the representation and rank statements. It does
not by itself establish continuous stochastic splittings, minimal
fixed-state widths over a parameter space, or novelty relative to the
classical partial-exchangeability literature.
