# Categorical Phenomenology: Subject, Mathematical Formulation, and Research Objectives

## 1. What categorical phenomenology is supposed to study

Categorical phenomenology is a proposed mathematical investigation of how structured objects, distinctions, relations, operations, and theories become available, warranted, and revisable through experience, interaction, and other admissible forms of evidence—and how the resulting constructions relate, compose, and transform across contexts.

This is a provisional characterization of the research programme, not an established mathematical definition of phenomenology.

This document proposes a research orientation and criteria for evaluating candidate formulations. It neither defines a completed mathematical subject nor selects a universal interface. [Contextual realization](../Desktop/eos-garden-workspace/eos-garden/theories-and-designs/categorical-phenomenology/contextual-realization-problem-note.tex) is one developed candidate strand whose scope and limitations must be assessed against the broader inquiry.

A central concern is the dependence of a proposed structure on how phenomena are accessed, distinguished, interpreted, and used: what the available presentations constrain, what the investigator or doctrine supplies, and how changing either alters the warranted structure. This focus motivates the programme but does not yet establish its boundary. Whether it distinguishes a useful class of problems remains an obligation to test; not every local theorem must address every part of that concern.

Its subject is broader than observational reconstruction. It includes the formation of the concepts and theoretical languages through which subsequent observations become meaningful.

The [method map](../Desktop/eos-garden-workspace/eos-garden/theories-and-designs/categorical-phenomenology/categorical-phenomenology-method-map.md) explicitly treats its observation-to-realization sequence as provisional rather than mandatory.

Neither this characterization nor the programme's existing examples establish that phenomenology has a single mathematical primitive. In particular, experience-to-structure, two-sided interaction, logical theory formation, predictive reconstruction, and contextual realization are candidate formulations, not interchangeable definitions of the subject.

Philosophical phenomenology supplies important motivations, but mathematical reconstruction of observations does not automatically capture intentionality, first-person experience, embodiment, or the other concerns of that tradition. The relationship between philosophical and mathematical phenomenology remains an interpretive obligation rather than an established equivalence.

## 2. Phenomenology and category theory have different roles

Phenomenology potentially encompasses questions belonging to several fundamental families:

| Question family | Concern within phenomenology |
|---|---|
| Structural | What structures arise, how do they compose, and what survives transformation? |
| Deductive | What follows from a proposed theory, and which concepts or laws can be defined or derived? |
| Epistemic | What is warranted by available experience or evidence, and what remains uncertain? |
| Interventional | How do actions change observed phenomena, and which interventions expose new distinctions? |
| Prescriptive | Which experiments, representations, or actions should be selected under specified objectives and constraints? |
| Computational | Can the relevant structures be constructed, identified, or verified effectively, and at what cost? |

This is a taxonomy of explanatory obligations, not a claim that mathematical phenomenology must independently solve every problem in these fields.

Category theory contributes structural organization, construction, comparison, and transport. It can establish universal properties, compositional laws, equivalences, exactness conditions, and obstructions. It can also construct new mathematical objects rather than merely organize existing ones.

Categorical structure alone does not supply a sampling model, consequence relation, intervention semantics, control objective, or computational guarantee. Such commitments must be specified and justified, whether expressed categorically or through another formalism. An integration must establish how the relevant constructions and guarantees interact.

Thus, categorical phenomenology is not simply category theory applied to observational data, nor is it a universal replacement for the mathematical disciplines it draws upon.

## 3. What a mathematical formulation of phenomenology needs

A useful formulation should begin with a class of phenomenological problems and their answer requirements, not with an already selected universal categorical construction.

At minimum, it needs the following distinctions. These are requirements for articulating and evaluating problems, not a proposed universal tuple of phenomenological primitives. Particular formulations may instantiate them differently or establish that some are inapplicable.

### A. Presentations, access, and warrant

A formulation should identify its starting presentations, judgments, constraints, or records; the operations available on them; and the grounds on which resulting claims are warranted. For an interaction-based formulation, this includes preparations, tests, response semantics, and actual experimental access.

Available records, complete ideal response laws, hypothetical experiments, and executable interventions are different inputs.

A useful candidate starting point is a two-sided testing correlation:

\[
H:P\times T\longrightarrow\Omega,
\]

where \(P\) represents preparations or presented situations, \(T\) represents tests or contexts, and \(\Omega\) is an appropriate response object.

However, this is one candidate interface, not a demonstrated universal primitive. Some problems require relational, probabilistic, temporal, higher-dimensional, or dynamically changing interaction structures.

The [two-sided realization proposal](../Desktop/eos-garden-workspace/eos-garden/theories-and-designs/categorical-phenomenology/towards-two-sided-context-sensitive-factorization-based-realization.md) develops this candidate through admissible factorizations and context-preservation laws.

Other formulations may begin with judgments and consequence relations, mathematical constructions and their constraints, dynamical processes, local compatibility data, or families of possible models. Their adequacy must be evaluated against the questions they are intended to answer.

The interface should not be made observational merely by redescribing every mathematical object or proof as an observation.

### B. A question–answer contract

A formulation must specify which questions it is intended to answer, what form their answers take, and what would warrant those answers.

Possible outputs include mathematical objects, theories, equivalence classes, proofs, predictive laws, identified sets, admissible actions, constructions, or obstructions.

These are not equivalent forms of success.

A canonical construction from an exact response law under a fixed doctrine may determine a realization up to a stated equivalence. It does not thereby establish that the available records determine that response law, justify the doctrine, or identify an underlying mechanism beyond the equivalence the theorem establishes. Establishing observational equivalence does not necessarily establish interventional equivalence. Finding an optimal representation under a selected objective does not establish that the representation is forced by the evidence.

The relevant notion of sufficiency is therefore **question-relative**.

A representation must support answers that remain admissible, with the required warrant, for the source situations it represents. For exact, uniquely answered questions, this requires that situations identified by the representation have the same answer. Where several answers are admissible, different source situations may share a valid answer without having identical sets of admissible answers. Preserving enough information to determine an answer does not itself supply a proof, effective algorithm, or justified interpretation.

No framework is sufficient merely because it answers every question expressible in its own language. The adequacy of the question language is itself open to examination.

### C. Structural doctrines and realization semantics

A problem must distinguish the information supplied from the mathematical structure introduced through a construction or inference doctrine.

Possible doctrines include algebraic generation, observational quotienting, logical closure, topological completion, probabilistic realization, causal modeling, and other operations.

They can produce fundamentally different outputs from the same initial presentation.

The [preliminary two-sided results](../Desktop/eos-garden-workspace/eos-garden/theories-and-designs/categorical-phenomenology/towards-two-sided-context-sensitive-factorization-based-realization-preliminary-results.md) [~] present a candidate comparison: under exact Boolean responses and specified algebraic doctrines, behavioral identification and formal concept completion arise through a common free-structure-plus-image construction. The same source argues that minimal stochastic latent realization does not generally share that universal characterization. These supporting arguments have not been independently verified for this formulation; their assumptions and checking obligations remain attached to the claims.

These differences are mathematically substantive.

A formulation must therefore identify the operations and laws associated with each doctrine, the properties its realization is required to preserve, and the additional assumptions needed to interpret its output.

It must also distinguish:

- constructing structure within a supplied language;
- selecting or identifying structure within a supplied family;
- introducing or revising the language, operations, or inference rules themselves.

Success at one level does not automatically establish success at another.

### D. Composition, contexts, and heterogeneous structure

Phenomenological problems may involve interactions that are sequential, parallel, adaptive, relational, probabilistic, local-to-global, or otherwise compositionally structured.

A reconstruction adequate for isolated observations need not remain adequate when used in a larger context.

For example, equality of individual measurement distributions need not determine a joint distribution. Equality of public observations need not permit substitution under interventions on hidden state. Locally realizable structures need not admit a compatible global realization.

The mathematical formulation must identify which contexts and compositions matter, what information they require, and whether the proposed constructions preserve their semantics.

This is also where heterogeneity becomes important.

Different problems may require different types of objects, morphisms, transformations, evidence, equivalence, and composition. A common ambient category does not establish that these structures can be meaningfully identified or that their results can be transported.

**Heterogeneity should be preserved where it can affect an intended answer, admissible operation, warrant, or compositional guarantee.** It should be reduced only through justified abstraction or comparison.

The objective is not unrestricted preservation of detail. It is to determine which distinctions can be discarded without invalidating the intended conclusions.

### E. Variation, formation, and revision

A mature formulation must accommodate changes in the conditions under which structures are constructed and interpreted.

These can include changes in evidence, available experiments, admissible interventions, theoretical language, inference doctrine, structural assumptions, or intended use.

Such changes are not equivalent.

Additional evidence can distinguish previously compatible hypotheses without changing the experiment language. New experimental capabilities can reveal distinctions inaccessible under the old repertoire. A change of mathematical doctrine can alter realizability while leaving observations unchanged. A change of language can introduce new objects, relations, or operations rather than merely supply more information about old ones.

A theory may also influence subsequent investigation by introducing concepts, questions, or operations that were not previously available.

Consequently, theory formation need not be represented by a single passage from fixed observations to a fixed mathematical object.

The formulation should investigate the relevant relationships among construction, interpretation, use, and revision, without presupposing a universal sequence or feedback mechanism.

### F. Warrant, stability, and limitations

Every substantive claim needs a warrant appropriate to its type.

Logical entailment requires a derivation or semantic consequence argument. Exact reconstruction requires the stated response law and construction hypotheses. Statistical identification requires an evidence and sampling model. Intervention claims require appropriate causal or operational assumptions. Computational claims require algorithms, bounds, or impossibility results.

A universal property does not justify its own application to a phenomenon. Likewise, a successful fit to observations does not establish a unique explanatory mechanism.

Stability must also be indexed to what is supposed to remain stable and under which changes.

Structural invariance, statistical consistency, robustness to perturbation, persistence under evidence extension, and continued explanatory usefulness are different properties. No general implication among them should be assumed.

The formulation must expose where its conclusions cease to hold, including changes of scale, observational access, interpretation, structural doctrine, and admissible composition.

## 4. Candidate formulations and their relationships

Several formulations deserve consideration without being promoted to universal foundations.

**Two-sided testing and realization** investigates structures arising from correlations between preparations and tests, including admissible intermediate factorizations and contextual preservation.

**Logical and model-theoretic formulation** investigates theories, satisfaction, consequence, definability, language change, and the relationship between observational or mathematical constraints and admissible models.

**Coalgebraic and dynamical formulation** investigates behavior, continuation, predictive equivalence, state reconstruction, and the effects of admissible actions.

**Statistical and information-theoretic formulation** investigates evidence, uncertainty, identifiability, sufficiency, experimental comparison, and quantitative information loss.

**Causal and operational formulation** investigates interventions, counterfactual distinctions, operational accessibility, and the preservation of behavior under controlled transformations.

**Local-to-global and geometric formulation** investigates contextual compatibility, descent, gluing, invariants, and obstructions to global realization.

These formulations overlap. Some already possess substantial categorical machinery. Others depend on complementary mathematical theories whose contents cannot be replaced by categorical notation.

They need not share a single kind of object, universal property, or notion of equivalence.

Their comparative study should determine whether they admit common constructions, directed translations, restricted compatibility principles, or irreducibly different mechanisms.

When comparing formulations for a shared problem, state what each receives as input, what it constructs or establishes, and which source-level questions and warrants survive the comparison. A proposed translation should expose its additional assumptions and information loss. When formulations address different questions, identify the connecting obligation rather than ranking them as interchangeable solutions.

The [method map](../Desktop/eos-garden-workspace/eos-garden/theories-and-designs/categorical-phenomenology/categorical-phenomenology-method-map.md) is a non-exhaustive source of candidate formulations and mathematical tools, not a definition of phenomenology or a mandatory research pipeline.

## 5. Research objectives

The research programme should retain several distinct but potentially interacting objectives.

### A. Clarify the mathematical subject

Develop and test formulations that distinguish genuinely phenomenological problems from arbitrary mathematical problems redescribed using phenomenological vocabulary.

Investigate what, if anything, different formulations share: kinds of presentation, access, dependence, meaning, contextual use, construction, or revision.

A single universal characterization is a possible result, not an assumption.

### B. Obtain substantive constructions

Construct structures, relations, operations, invariants, realizations, or theories from independently specified inputs and assumptions.

Distinguish what the construction generates from what its premises already supply.

Establish the relevant universal, extremal, inferential, operational, or quantitative properties, together with genuine failure cases.

Formation of theoretical languages and concepts remains a major open direction, but not every generative result must solve that problem.

### C. Explain relationships among heterogeneous formulations

Investigate when different methods agree, when one translates into another, which information or structure is lost, and which comparisons are obstructed.

A successful comparison should establish a consequence beyond placing existing constructions in common notation.

The strongest justified outcome may be a shared mechanism, a structured family of doctrines, a partial translation theory, or a classification of unavoidable differences.

### D. Connect structural results with complementary explanations

Determine how categorical constructions interact with deductive, epistemic, interventional, computational, and other relevant frameworks.

A proposed integration should identify the respective inputs, outputs, warrants, and compatibility conditions.

Locally valid results need not assemble into a valid global explanation. Such assembly requires its own justification.

### E. Investigate formation, stabilization, and revision

Study how structures and theoretical organizations may be introduced, justified, used, and changed under new evidence, operations, mathematical constraints, or contexts.

Distinguish canonical construction, evidential identification, practical selection, and language formation.

Investigate the conditions under which a structure remains adequate or must be refined or replaced, rather than presupposing one universal theory-formation mechanism.

### F. Establish meaningful generality and limits

Test substantive constructions and comparison principles across genuinely different settings.

The purpose is not to accumulate examples but to discover which assumptions are essential, which mathematical results transport, and where different semantics force different answers.

Failure of a proposed unification can itself be significant if the obstruction is proved at the claimed scope.

Neither a successful local construction nor a collection of similar examples establishes a general theory of phenomenology.

## 6. What constitutes research progress

Three judgments must remain separate:

1. **Mathematical success:** Has a stated construction, theorem, comparison, or obstruction been established under its declared assumptions?
2. **Explanatory contribution:** What does the result clarify about the phenomenological problem, its available answers, or the relationships among formulations?
3. **Programme significance:** Does the result advance an important unresolved question, alter the organizing account, or justify a particular subsequent investigation?

The first does not imply the other two.

Conversely, a substantial synthesis of established mathematics may have explanatory significance without supporting a claim of theorem-level novelty.

A research contract should therefore maintain a stable overarching inquiry while permitting revisable local mathematical commitments.

Local investigations require explicit hypotheses, outputs, proof obligations, source comparisons, and stopping conditions. Their successful completion must be credited at the scope actually established.

At major research decisions, the programme must separately assess which overarching question has become better understood, which competing formulations remain viable, and what mathematical or explanatory obligation remains unresolved.

The next theorem should not be selected merely because it is the nearest tractable extension of the preceding result.

Neither should every local result be required to demonstrate immediate unification across multiple domains. Such a demand can distort otherwise significant investigations.

What is required is accountability for integration: local results cannot indefinitely substitute for the comparative and explanatory achievements the programme claims to seek.

The programme should retain the following mathematical safeguards: supplied/derived boundaries, explicit semantics, source discipline, nonvacuous constructions, native admissibility where claimed, and precise evidence and resource assumptions.

Diagnostic examples and preferred constructions should remain revisable. Passing a fixed suite does not establish that the underlying phenomenological questions have been answered.

## 7. The central unresolved questions

The foundational problems can be summarized as follows.

**Subject.** What mathematically distinguishes a phenomenological problem, and how can that distinction be made substantive without excluding legitimate but currently unforeseen formulations?

**Construction.** Under which assumptions can meaningful structures, concepts, relations, operations, or theories be constructed or identified from the relevant presentations and constraints?

**Comparison.** What mathematical relationships connect heterogeneous constructions, and which differences prevent stronger identification or transport?

**Warrant.** What justifies the resulting structures and conclusions, and how does that justification depend on evidence, interpretation, operations, theoretical commitments, and intended use?

**Composition.** When do locally adequate descriptions or constructions remain adequate when assembled into larger systems or subjected to new contexts?

**Revision.** How can newly introduced structures change the available questions, operations, and interpretations, and when do those changes preserve or invalidate earlier conclusions?

**Scope.** How much common mathematics can these problems support without erasing distinctions essential to their meaning or solution?

These questions are neither an exhaustive definition nor a compulsory checklist. They identify significant unresolved obligations that a research programme bearing the name *categorical phenomenology* may reasonably investigate.

## 8. Conclusion

Categorical phenomenology should not be defined prematurely by a particular reconstruction procedure, testing interface, categorical doctrine, or theory-formation mechanism.

Its mathematical subject remains an open foundational question.

The programme should investigate phenomenological problems through formulations that preserve their relevant meanings, admissible operations, evidential and mathematical warrants, and answer requirements.

Category theory can contribute substantive constructions and a systematic account of how different formulations compose, compare, transform, or resist unification. Complementary frameworks supply additional mathematical and explanatory content.

**The guiding research objective is to determine which structural principles genuinely organize phenomenological problems, what those principles enable one to construct or establish, and where their explanatory authority ends.**

A successful programme may produce a unified theory, a structured family of interoperating theories, or a rigorous account of irreducible differences. None of those outcomes should be selected before the mathematics warrants it.

The research contract should preserve that openness without sacrificing mathematical rigor, explanatory accountability, or the obligation to obtain substantive results.

# A few example motivating problems

The multi-robot scenario is a particularly useful motivating problem because it forces heterogeneous observations, representations, reasoning, interventions, and coordination into the same system. A shared world model cannot be judged solely by whether it reconciles sensor data; it must support subsequent actions, preserve relevant uncertainty, and remain revisable.

But a single unified world model should be a possible outcome, not a premise. Some systems may require a collection of partial, overlapping, or mutually incompatible models.

Here are four motivating problems that expose different aspects of the larger research programme.

## 1. Heterogeneous robots with a defeasible shared world model

Consider several robots exploring and operating in a changing environment.

A drone has visual and thermal observations. A ground robot has LiDAR and an occupancy map. A manipulator has tactile feedback and an object-level model. A planning agent represents rooms, doors, and tasks symbolically. Controllers use different dynamics, assumptions, and planning horizons.

The robots must coordinate, but their representations need not share the same objects, state spaces, or semantics.

One robot reports that a corridor is clear. Another reports an obstruction. These observations could differ because of time, viewpoint, sensor error, inconsistent coordinate frames, or a genuine environmental change.

The shared model must preserve these possibilities until something justifies distinguishing or eliminating them.

The motivating problem:

> How can heterogeneous agents construct, maintain, compare, and revise a shared account of their environment that is sufficient for coordinated action, without requiring a single complete or uniquely identified world representation?

The key research questions concern what information can legitimately be shared, which local distinctions survive translation, when locally valid models admit a compatible global interpretation, and whether actions justified by the shared model remain valid in the original agents' operational semantics.

Defeasibility introduces additional structure. A model may need to retract a claim, weaken its confidence, distinguish stale evidence from contradictory evidence, or revise the concepts used to describe an object. These are not all equivalent to Bayesian conditioning.

A concrete obstruction: suppose two possible routes each have a marginal probability \(1/2\) of being passable. Those marginals alone do not determine the probability that at least one route is passable:

\[ P(A\cup B)= \begin{cases} 1/2 & \text{perfectly correlated}\\ 3/4 & \text{independent}\\ 1 & \text{perfectly anticorrelated}. \end{cases} \]

With these marginals, the union probability can range from \(1/2\) to \(1\). Independence is an additional assumption about the joint distribution, not a consequence of the marginals. A shared system that silently assumes it can authorize an action its evidence does not warrant.

The categorical problem is not merely data fusion. It concerns compatible translations, composition of operations, preservation of operational guarantees, and obstructions to global reconstruction.

## 2. An autonomous scientific laboratory and its evolving world twin

Consider a scientific laboratory containing spectroscopy instruments, microscopy, chemical assays, robotic manipulators, environmental sensors, and automated experimental equipment.

Its models might include differential equations, probabilistic models, chemical reaction networks, simulation models, causal hypotheses, and learned surrogate predictors.

These models describe overlapping aspects of the same experimental system, but at different scales and through different mathematical languages.

Suppose an unexpected spectral peak appears.

It might indicate a new chemical intermediate, an instrument artifact, contamination, or inadequacy of the existing reaction model.

A world twin that merely updates the parameters of its existing model may be unable to represent the correct explanation. The problem may require introducing a new species, reaction, measurement model, or experimental distinction.

The motivating problem:

> How can a scientific world twin construct and revise explanatory structures from heterogeneous evidence, maintain competing theoretical accounts, and design experiments that discriminate among them while remaining compatible with the laboratory's actual operations?

This involves more than keeping a simulated state synchronized with measurements.

The laboratory must distinguish parameter estimation from model selection, model selection from language revision, and observational adequacy from causal adequacy.

A model may predict the current measurements accurately but fail under a change in temperature or reagent concentration. The twin must therefore account for how models behave under admissible interventions.

A categorical framework could investigate relationships among the different model structures, how their predictions compose, and under what conditions conclusions can be transferred between them.

Probability, system identification, causal inference, and experimental design would supply additional obligations.

This example directly tests whether categorical phenomenology can contribute to scientific theory formation, rather than only reconstructing states inside a supplied theory.

## 3. A heterogeneous environmental or ecological world model

Consider a watershed observed through satellites, ground sensors, weather stations, drones, and laboratory water samples.

Different models describe atmospheric conditions, hydrological flow, soil absorption, chemical transport, ecological populations, and infrastructure.

The models differ in scale, mathematical structure, resolution, and assumptions.

The motivating problem:

> Under what conditions can local, heterogeneous models support reliable global explanations and predictions, and how should incompatibilities between scales, assumptions, and observations be represented?

A hydrological model may produce spatially averaged quantities while an ecological model requires local concentrations. Translating between them can destroy information essential to downstream predictions.

A collection of locally consistent models may also fail to admit a global realization satisfying their shared constraints.

This scenario emphasizes locality, gluing, multiscale abstraction, uncertainty propagation, and model composition.

It provides a natural test for whether reconstruction and model integration commute with changes in resolution or observational access.

## 4. A mathematical theory-forming system

This is a different kind of motivating problem, and an important control against defining phenomenology exclusively through physical instrumentation.

Suppose a system encounters examples, conjectures, proofs, counterexamples, constructions, and unexpected correspondences across several mathematical areas.

Its task is not merely to prove consequences in a supplied formal language. It may need to introduce new definitions, operations, equivalence relations, or theories that organize the encountered mathematics.

The motivating problem:

> How can mathematical phenomena constrain the introduction of new concepts and theoretical structures, and what makes a resulting theory explanatory rather than an arbitrary formal presentation?

For example, a family of constructions may suggest a universal property. Different mathematical theories may recover the same construction under different assumptions. A counterexample may demonstrate that a proposed abstraction discarded a necessary distinction.

The relevant evidence is often deductive rather than statistical.

This problem tests whether the programme's account of formation and warrant extends beyond experimental reconstruction without treating proofs, measurements, and theoretical choices as interchangeable.

## The common mathematical challenge

These examples are not simply four applications of a single data-integration problem.

They differ in what counts as evidence, what kinds of objects are constructed, which operations are admissible, and what constitutes a successful explanation.

Nevertheless, they suggest a common research question:

How can heterogeneous, partial, and revisable accounts of phenomena be constructed, related, and used together while preserving the distinctions, warrants, and compositional properties required by their intended uses?

A categorical approach might provide constructions and comparisons among these accounts. But it should not assume that every account is a model of one hidden world object or that every collection of local models admits a canonical global completion.

The strongest research outcomes could include a genuine common construction, a family of interoperable but different doctrines, or a precise obstruction explaining why stronger integration is impossible.

## What the motivating examples test

The robot scenario foregrounds preservation of action requirements across heterogeneous representations. The laboratory scenario foregrounds revision of explanatory mechanisms and theoretical vocabulary. The environmental scenario foregrounds compatibility across scales and the information needed for model composition. The mathematical theory-formation scenario tests whether the account preserves deductive warrant without reducing it to empirical observation.

These are complementary diagnostic roles, not an established ranking of difficulty or research value. Selecting a starting case depends on which unresolved obligation the investigation intends to settle.

A useful initial research question for the robot case would be:

> Given heterogeneous agents with partially overlapping observations, model languages, and admissible actions, when does there exist a shared, revisable representation sufficient for a specified class of coordinated tasks? When such a representation does not exist, what weaker structures—compatible local models, directed translations, explicit uncertainty, or task-relative abstractions—can support justified coordination?

Here, sufficient must mean preservation of explicitly specified task-relevant observations, predictions, and action requirements. It should not mean complete reconstruction of the environment.

That formulation permits a positive construction, an exact obstruction, or a task-relative result. It also avoids presupposing a unique world twin. To connect it to this programme, the investigation should identify which distinctions and structures are constructed from the agents' access and constraints, which are supplied by the integration architecture, and what warrants the resulting choices.

The scientific-lab version could then challenge the same organizing ideas in a setting where the theoretical vocabulary and explanatory mechanisms themselves may need to change.

Together, the two would provide a demanding test of whether categorical phenomenology is developing substantive mathematics across distinct families of questions, rather than merely a general formalism for connecting models.
