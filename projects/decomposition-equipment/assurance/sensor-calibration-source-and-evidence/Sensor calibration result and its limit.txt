Sensor calibration result and its limit
30 October 2026

Abstract

In this specified two-sensor model, two calibration probes are necessary and
sufficient to get the worst-case whole-run failure probability below 4.5%.
They cost up to two time units; counting production, the run needs a deadline
of five. Under the stated energy bookkeeping it needs four energy units.
The exact optimum is 4.4916159744%. One probe cannot meet the target.

Existing decision and hypothesis-testing methods solve this case. No new
composition theorem is needed. The important qualification is that the model
already tells us how calibration relates to production. If that link can
secretly reverse, every permitted calibration record is compatible with two
opposite production choices. Then no number of these probes can beat 4.9204%
worst-case failure. This locates a genuine missing premise: the calibration
to production link must be trusted or tested by a different experiment. The
results are exact for the stated model, not measurements of a physical device.


1  What the experiment assumes

There are two sensor modes, labelled 0 and 1, and one persistent unknown bit,
theta. Mode r is aligned when r equals theta; otherwise it is split. Theta is
fixed before the controller's private random choices, not chosen after seeing
them.

A safe calibration fixture supplies a known target, making the report-fault
bits A and B observable. With independent primitive fault bits U1, U2, U3,
the given model is

    A = U1 OR U2
    B = A                 in the aligned mode
    B = U2 OR U3          in the split mode.

During calibration each primitive fault has probability 0.2. The resulting
probabilities are

    Observed pair       Aligned       Split
    00                   0.640        0.512
    11                   0.360        0.232
    01                   0            0.128
    10                   0            0.128

A disagreement proves the tested mode is split. Agreement does not prove it
is aligned. Primitive draws are fresh on each call, but theta persists.

Calibration is assumed harmless and costs one time and one energy unit.
Production uses fresh primitive faults with probability 0.02, two sensor polls,
and one execution. It costs three time units. Its two-unit energy cost is a
bookkeeping convention, not measured evidence. Failure means either poll is
faulty, or the run aborts or misses its deadline. There is no additional
execution fault in this model. Production supplies no usable information
about whether its payload is faulty. No alternative actions or concurrent
execution are available.

The fault rates, source templates, safe calibration fixture and unchanged
calibration to production routing are supplied assumptions. The controller
learns about theta, not those assumptions. For integer budgets D and E, where
D is the deadline and E is energy, the maximum probe count is
n = min(D - 3, E - 2). If production alone does not fit, the mission fails.


2  The optimal policy and its actual cost

Production in the aligned mode depends on two distinct fault sources; the
split mode depends on three. Thus their failure probabilities are

    a = 1 - 0.98^2 = 0.0396
    b = 1 - 0.98^3 = 0.058808.

The exact minimum worst-case failure probability with at most n probes is

    R(n) = 0.0396 + 0.009604 * 0.744^n.

This minimizes failure over all allowed policies and takes the worse of the
two fixed values of theta. It is not an average-case guarantee based on an
assumed distribution of theta.

    Max probes   Deadline   Energy   Optimal failure probability
    0            3          2        0.049204
    1            4          3        0.046745376
    2            5          4        0.044916159744
    3            6          5        0.043555222849536

An optimal controller is simple:

    1. Choose mode 0 or 1 with a private fair coin.
    2. Probe that same mode until the first disagreement or the budget ends.
    3. On disagreement, switch modes and stop probing.
       Otherwise keep the initially chosen mode.
    4. Complete production in that mode.

For either fixed theta, the chance of choosing the wrong production mode is
0.5 * 0.744^n. Each extra probe reduces this residual mode error by 25.6%.
The fair initial choice gives the same total failure probability in both
worlds, making the worst-case guarantee attainable.

With the two-probe budget, early stopping uses 1.872 probes on average, so
expected total time is 4.872 and expected energy is 3.872. The worst-case
costs remain five and four. The guarantee counts the learning before acting;
it does not grant free identification or discard unsuccessful runs.


3  Enough information is not complete identification

After this two-probe policy, the phase is still ambiguous with probability
0.776768. Both values of theta remain possible whenever all observed pairs
agree. Nevertheless, the policy meets the whole-run target in each fixed
world. A useful decision can therefore be certified without fully recovering
the hidden arrangement.

The 4.4916% guarantee is assessed before the run, including its random choices
and calibration outcomes. It is not a fresh 4.5% guarantee conditional on
every observed history. In particular, we cannot keep only favorable records
and reset the risk budget.

The controller need only retain its chosen mode, probe count and whether a
disagreement occurred. It can discard the distinction between 00 and 11 even
though their likelihoods differ. This is sufficient for this decision, not
a reconstruction of everything the observations could reveal.

The source accounting also matters. Each sensor separately has operational
fault probability 0.0396. Adding those marginal bounds gives 0.0792, too loose
to certify the target. That is a failed certificate, not proof the mission is
impossible. Counting the distinct shared sources gives the sharper joint
values a and b. Here the hazard is an OR of failures, so sharing a fault source
reduces that union probability; shared noise is not automatically worse.


4  What happens if the calibration link is not known

Now allow one extra persistent unknown bit, gamma. Calibration still uses
theta, but the production phase is theta XOR gamma. Gamma 0 means the link
is unchanged; gamma 1 means it reverses. There are four possible fixed worlds.

For a fixed theta, the two gamma values give identical calibration records
under every permitted policy, however many probes it takes. Yet the preferred
production modes are opposite. Even perfect knowledge of the calibration
phase cannot tell the controller which production mode is better.

The exact worst-case optimum is then

    (a + b) / 2 = 0.049204,

for every probe budget. A fair production-mode choice without calibration
already attains it. Each world individually has a 0.0396-risk choice, but
no single permitted learning policy can reliably select it across all four
worlds. The unchanged-link two-probe policy would instead have failure
probability 0.053491840256 in the reversed-link worlds.

This is an information limit, not a shortage of clever computation. More of
the same records cannot resolve a distinction that never affects them.


5  What this establishes

The positive case reduces to a finite partially observed decision problem
with known observation laws, coupled to an explicit local fault-union check.
Standard methods solve it exactly. Related output-feedback abstraction work
formalizes control from observed histories [1]; probability-sensitive POMDP
abstraction already supports controller refinement with satisfaction bounds
[2]. Those papers provide background, not this case's numerical proof. Here
the explicit finite calculation makes approximate abstraction unnecessary.
The compact controller requires no online optimization. Declared operation
costs are counted, but no measured hardware timing or general computation-cost
certificate is claimed.

The negative case identifies the missing physical link precisely. It does not
show a need for a new composition theorem. Nor does the positive case solve
the stronger problem of learning unknown likelihoods, discovering arbitrary
source structures, or validating a physical calibration bridge affordably.

The two-probe result also has little numerical slack: it is only
0.000083840256 below the 0.045 limit. Exact assumed rates support the result;
unquantified rate or model errors do not. A physical safety claim would need
evidence and uncertainty bounds for those assumptions.

Verification combined exact rational dynamic programming through six probes,
replay over primitive fault outcomes in each world, and enumeration of 11,686
deterministic policy trees through two probes. These use three
information-distinct outcome classes, with repeated lower-depth policies
retained. An independent audit rederived the result and reran the checks.
The general optimality and unknown-link conclusions follow from the short
proofs below, not from treating finite computation as an all-budget proof.


Short proof

For either probe mode, the overlap of its two observation distributions is

    c = sum over outcomes of min(P0, P1) = 0.512 + 0.232 = 0.744.

At any history with likelihood weights x and y, any next probe obeys

    sum min(x P0, y P1) >= c * min(x, y).

Consequently an adaptive n-probe experiment retains transcript overlap at
least c^n. Equal-prior binary testing error is half that overlap. This average
error lower-bounds worst-case error, even when actions use raw histories and
private randomness. Early-stopping policies can be padded with ignored probes
for this lower-bound argument. Abort counts as failure and cannot improve on
feasible production, whose failure probability is at most b < 1.

The displayed policy errs only if its initial fair choice is wrong and all
n split-mode probes agree. That probability is c^n / 2 in either world. It
therefore attains the lower bound. Fresh production faults give

    R(n) = a + (b - a) * c^n / 2.

For the unknown-link case, pair the two gamma worlds at any fixed theta.
Their calibration transcripts, stopping decisions and final action
distributions are identical. Any production choice has risk a in one world
and b in the other. Their risks therefore sum to a + b; branches that abort
or miss the deadline only increase this sum. At least one world has risk
at least (a + b) / 2. A fair production-mode choice attains equality in every
world, proving the stated optimum for every budget that permits production.
